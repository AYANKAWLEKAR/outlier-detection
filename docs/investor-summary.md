# Agent Action Firewall: what we're building and why

AI agents are moving from chatting to doing. They issue refunds, update CRM records, send email and post to Slack on a company's behalf. Today's safety tools mostly try to catch a bad *prompt* before it reaches the model, and attackers keep finding new wording that gets past them. We take a different approach: we watch what each agent actually *does*. Our engine learns every agent's normal behaviour: how large its refunds usually are, who it usually emails, which steps it always takes before making a change, and how fast it works. It flags any action that falls far outside that pattern, such as a $4,000 refund from an agent that normally refunds $37, an email to a domain it has never contacted, or a write to a record it never looked up. It needs no prior knowledge of the tool, the attack or the prompt, which means it can protect agents and tools we have never seen. It checks each action in a fraction of a millisecond and explains in plain terms why it blocked something.

The plan covers the first two build phases over about seven weeks. Phase one builds the detection engine and tests it against a large simulated workload: 20 different agent "personalities" and eight kinds of attack. It has to catch at least 90% of the attacks it targets while rarely raising a false alarm on normal work. Phase two moves from simulation to reality. We run open-source AI agents against real sandboxed business systems (Stripe in test mode, a Salesforce developer org, Slack and email), try to trick them, and record everything. The result is a public benchmark of more than 40,000 real agent actions and 600 attacks, with our engine's scores published next to standard alternatives, including asking a frontier AI model to judge each action. That benchmark is the strategic asset. It turns "trust us" into a number anyone can verify, it sets the yardstick competitors will be measured against, and it gives us the evidence base to sell an enforcement product to every company putting agents into production. The direct cost of these two phases is small: under roughly $1,000 in AI usage, with all third-party systems on free test tiers.
